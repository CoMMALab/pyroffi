\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate our method in two settings. \textbf{IOC} (\S\ref{sec:exp_ioc}) recovers cost weights from demonstrations on single trajectory-optimization segments---first on 2D benchmarks with known ground-truth costs, then on a 7-DOF manipulator.
\textbf{IOSP} (\S\ref{sec:exp_iosp}) extends recovery to composed task skeletons spanning IK through multi-segment manipulation, on synthetic domains and on human teleoperation.

All experiments use JAX with double precision (\texttt{JAX\_ENABLE\_X64=1}) and implicit differentiation through the trajectory-optimization solution via the implicit function theorem. The outer loop fits cost weights~$\theta$ by minimizing the rollout error of $x^*(\theta)$ against demonstrations, using Adam with analytic gradients from the implicit adjoint. We compare against finite differences (FD), CMA-ES, unrolled differentiation, and Inverse KKT where applicable.

\subsection{IOC: single-segment inverse optimal control}
\label{sec:exp_ioc}

\subsubsection{Scaling with cost dimension}
\label{sec:exp_scaling}

We measure how reconstruction cost and compute scale with the number of cost features~$K$ on the 7-DOF Panda.
As $K$ grows, the sensitivity Gram $G = J^\top J$ becomes increasingly rank-deficient: the identifiable subspace has dimension~$r \leq K$, and directions in the null space are unrecoverable from demonstration data alone.
We report cost regret (the suboptimality of the recovered cost under the true weights) rather than raw parameter error, since weight error along low-eigenvalue directions does not correspond to behavioural error.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/fig1_scaling.png}
    \caption{Scaling of implicit-adjoint IOC with cost dimension~$K$ on the 7-DOF Panda. Left: reconstruction error (cost regret) as a function of~$K$. Right: wall-clock time. The implicit adjoint scales gracefully; FD and CMA-ES both scale with the number of forward solves.}
    \label{fig:scaling_cost_parameters}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Cost field reconstruction}
\label{sec:exp_field}

To visualise recovery quality beyond scalar metrics, we use a 2D benchmark with a known cost field defined by a mixture of radial basis functions.
The solver observes trajectories through this field and attempts to reconstruct the cost landscape.
We test two regimes: a wide (unimodal) field and a narrow (multimodal) field, each under increasing demonstration noise~$\sigma$.

\subsubsection{Noise robustness on the 7-DOF arm}
\label{sec:exp_noise_robot}

We ablate noise sensitivity on the Panda by adding zero-mean Gaussian noise to the demonstrated trajectories and measuring reconstruction quality as a function of~$\sigma$.
The implicit adjoint degrades smoothly; at moderate noise levels it still recovers cost weights that produce trajectories within millimetres of the demonstration.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/fig4_noise_robot.png}
    \caption{Reconstruction error on the 7-DOF Panda under increasing demonstration noise~$\sigma$. The implicit adjoint degrades gracefully; CMA-ES is competitive at low noise but more variable.}
    \label{fig:noise_ablation}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{KKT seeding of the implicit fit}
\label{sec:exp_kkt_seed}

The Inverse KKT estimator solves $B(c)\,\theta = 0$ at the demonstration to obtain a closed-form weight estimate.
While this estimate is inconsistent when the demonstration is noisy or the problem has inequality constraints, it provides a strong initialisation for the implicit-adjoint fit.
We compare random initialisation against KKT-seeded initialisation across both field regimes and noise levels.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/fig5_kkt_seed_ablation.png}
    \caption{KKT seeding of the implicit-adjoint fit. At low noise the KKT seed provides orders-of-magnitude improvement; at higher noise ($\sigma \geq 0.02$) with more demonstrations ($R=4$), the benefit diminishes as the implicit fit converges regardless.}
    \label{fig:kkt_seed}
\end{figure}

\subsection{IOSP: inverse optimal control on composed task skeletons}
\label{sec:exp_iosp}

Single-segment IOC assumes the planner solves one optimisation problem. Real manipulation tasks are composed: an IK solve selects a grasp configuration, then chained trajectory-optimisation segments (approach, grasp, transport, place) execute the task.
IOSP inverts this entire skeleton by differentiating through the composed forward map---IK through all segments---using the same implicit adjoint, with literal (undetached) data flow between stages.

\subsubsection{Identifiability under composition}
\label{sec:exp_identifiability}

When cost features are shared across segments, the sensitivity Gram of the composed problem can be rank-deficient even when each segment's Gram is full-rank.
We decompose recovery error into its identifiable and null-space components using the eigendecomposition of the composed Gram.
This analysis reveals that apparent recovery failures are often projections onto unidentifiable directions rather than optimisation failures.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/fig_eigenspectrum.png}
    \caption{Eigenspectrum of the composed sensitivity Gram on the pick-and-place skeleton. The sharp spectral drop separates identifiable directions (left of the gap) from the null space. Recovery error concentrates almost entirely in the null component.}
    \label{fig:eigenspectrum}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Multistart robustness}
\label{sec:exp_multistart}

The composed forward map is non-convex, with basins induced by IK branch selection.
We run 9 candidates (3 IK branches $\times$ 3 cost initialisations) and select the winner on training loss.
On a held-out scene at $2\times$ displacement, the selected candidate generalises: training-loss selection matches held-out selection on the majority of seeds, with regret below $0.2$~mm when it does not.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/fig2_multistart.png}
    \caption{Multistart robustness on the composed pick-and-place task. Each panel shows candidate trajectories at the final outer step; the selected winner (training loss) closely tracks the demonstration on both the fit and held-out scenes. Branch dominates start in determining the basin.}
    \label{fig:multistart}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Domain evaluation}
\label{sec:exp_domains}

We evaluate IOSP on three synthetic manipulation domains of increasing complexity: pick-and-place, tetris-style packing, and block stacking.
Each domain uses a known ground-truth~$\theta^*$; demonstrations are rollouts of the composed planner at~$\theta^*$.
We report held-out end-effector RMSE against a uniform-weight baseline and an oracle (rollout at the true weights), as well as normalised cost regret and context-projected parameter error.

\subsubsection{Human teleoperation}
\label{sec:exp_teleop}

We evaluate IOSP on 10 teleoperated pick-and-place episodes recorded on an FR3 through a GELLO leader.
No ground-truth~$\theta^*$ exists; the claim is purely behavioural---does the fitted planner reproduce held-out episodes?
We fit on 8 episodes and evaluate on the remaining 2, using 12 candidates (4 IK branches $\times$ 3 cost starts).
The IK grasp and release orientations are anchored to each episode's demonstrated configuration; without this anchoring, orientation mismatch dominates the residual and masks the cost recovery signal.

We compare implicit differentiation against finite differences, CMA-ES, and unrolled differentiation.
